# 01 — Complete Chronological Story Flow

**Working title:** **RISE FROM ZERO**  
**Core tagline:** *Lost Everything. Starting From Nothing. Racing For Everything.*  
**Story principle:** A continuous emotional narrative, **not scattered independent races**. This is a **beat-by-beat reconstruction** of agreed story facts, not a fabricated finished screenplay. Where user has not specified a scene's details, its exact contents are [TBD].

## Quick-read plot — one uninterrupted thread
Two childhood friends are born into poor circumstances. They learn how cars work, learn to drive and start dreaming of a better future while spending time in a garage. Their shared passion moves from simple mechanical work into street racing. The early victories belong to both; the hope is that friendship and skill can overcome poverty. As the spotlight grows, so does ambition. A woman who loves the protagonist becomes a meaningful presence in his racing life; she is not simply a trophy at the finish line. Then, at a crucial point in their rise, the friend betrays the protagonist. The betrayal lands the protagonist in prison, while the former friend moves ahead and builds a powerful racing empire. The relationship with the woman becomes complicated by the rival's pressure, but she does not stop caring about the protagonist; she offers concealed support and clues. Eventually the protagonist comes out of prison without his old position, money or access to the racing power he helped build. He has to restart with limited resources and earn his way back in the world that has changed around him. Through tougher expert races, repeated challenges, police risks, losses, mechanical upgrades and new vehicles, he rises through the ranks. His racing behavior and victories affect how other characters and the woman respond. The journey ends in a direct confrontation with the old friend and the empire built after the betrayal; the final consequences and endings should reflect the player's actions and performance across the career. This is the **required emotional spine** of the game.

## Full chronological beat board

### PROLOGUE — “Before the World Knew Our Names”
**When:** childhood/early youth, before meaningful racing fame.  
**Thematic purpose:** Establish poverty, trust, dreams and the garage as home.

1. [CONFIRMED] Introduce **two childhood friends**, both poor. The relationship must be visibly close and long-standing.
2. [CONFIRMED] They **learn driving and car mechanics in a garage**. The garage is a recognizable foundational environment, not an arbitrary setting.
3. [WORKING implementation] Interactive learning beats can teach accelerate/brake, steering, changing gears and basic repair while the friendship is shown in scenes. The *existence, order and dialogue of specific tutorial missions are TBD*.
4. [CONFIRMED] The love interest is present in the **startup/loading/intro presentation** and continues to matter through the game. Placement of her first in-story meeting is [TBD].

**Player experience:** emotional introduction + low-stakes foundational driving.  
**Do not invent:** first-car make/model, garage owner, parents' biographies, ages or character names.

### ACT I — “We Rise Together”
**When:** early street-racing career.  
**Thematic purpose:** Make the shared dream valuable enough that its loss hurts.

1. The two friends transition from working on cars to participating in street races and **building a racing name together**.
2. The player discovers the emerging racing community, early experts, reputation, and how a better setup matters to driving.
3. The girlfriend/love interest takes an active guiding role rather than appearing only at romance cutscenes. Her demeanor is linked to the player's progress and results.
4. The friendship and rising stakes are depicted in short travel/dialogue moments and longer milestone cinematics.
5. [WORKING] Early low-rank races and inexpensive cars can coincide with this act, but the owner **has not assigned exact ranks** to the pre-betrayal phase.

**Player experience:** shared achievements, the garage becoming a source of pride, life beginning to change.

### ACT II — “The Crack in Trust”
**When:** rising fame; tension before betrayal.  
**Thematic purpose:** Prepare a betrayal that makes emotional sense.

1. [CONFIRMED] The friend eventually betrays the protagonist. The betrayal is not an unexplained disconnected event.
2. [WORKING] Dialogue, events and subtle reactions can foreshadow jealousy or power struggles **only if approved**; no motive is confirmed yet.
3. [CONFIRMED] The girlfriend/love interest becomes associated with the **betrayal mystery**; her visible situation does **not** mean she voluntarily abandoned the protagonist.
4. [TBD] The precise cause of the fall (framing, criminal charge, evidence, which race, who calls police, material consequences and how exactly the rival uses coercion) has **not been established**. Writers must not choose an irreversible explanation without a recorded owner decision.

**Player experience:** escalation in emotional pressure and damaged trust leading to the decisive event.

### ACT III — “Betrayed / Behind Bars”
**When:** betrayal and incarceration.  
**Thematic purpose:** Mark a genuinely costly fall rather than a temporary race penalty.

1. [CONFIRMED] The protagonist **goes to prison because of his friend's betrayal**.
2. [CONFIRMED] The betrayer moves ahead to build his **racing empire** while the protagonist is out.
3. [CONFIRMED] The girlfriend still loves the protagonist; she is **under pressure/coercion from the rival**, not simply rewritten into a villain, and secretly attempts to support the protagonist's eventual return.
4. [TBD] Trial details, length of imprisonment, any prison gameplay and the specific betrayal reveal are **not written**. Do not conflate this *story imprisonment* with ordinary “busted by police” sessions later.
5. A long, pivotal cutscene should convey the loss of trust, status and future; exact lines/visual staging remain [TBD].

**Player experience:** a major narrative rupture, followed by an intentional temporal transition.

### ACT IV — “Zero Means Zero”
**When:** release and first days back.  
**Thematic purpose:** Convert story loss into concrete gameplay scarcity.

1. [CONFIRMED] He **gets out of prison** and has to **start racing life from nothing**.
2. The old friend's empire now stands in the way. Returning is not an instant restoration of previous garages, cars or rank.
3. [CONFIRMED] A **small starter garage**, few affordable vehicles (initial capacity concept: 1–2 cars), restricted parts, low career rank and early challenges form the comeback experience.
4. The woman can supply **clues and covert guidance** while constrained by the antagonist; scenes must respect the established emotional continuity.
5. [WORKING] One initial compact playable sector/garage can act as the first technical vertical slice; it does not mean the fictional world only has one district.
6. [TBD] Exact starting cash, model of first car, whether one car is free, earliest race, first safehouse, exact rank after release.

**Player experience:** sharp contrast with the previous success; rebuilding must be earned.

### ACT V — “Climb Through the City”
**When:** the bulk of the gameplay career.  
**Thematic purpose:** Make every earned advance narratively relevant.

1. [CONFIRMED] Compete through the **fifteen-rank rival framework**. Every rank's expert has **five fixed races**; **at least three wins** unlock advancement subject to relevant boss rules. Bosses can require **all specified boss races**; exact count for each is [TBD]. Refer to 03.
2. [CONFIRMED] Races vary in type, city, terrain and time determined by the challenger, often with changing weather, traffic and police dynamics. No “every race is daytime.”
3. Build earnings, reputation, vehicles, tuned engines/gearboxes/nitrous/body parts, and garage capacity **within level locks**. Cars require physical/performance compatibility.
4. [CONFIRMED] Police pursuit and busted consequences may interrupt this climb but **do not erase the campaign**. Repeat interrupted challenges where required.
5. [CONFIRMED] After races, **girlfriend, rivals and others show emotion/reactions based on observed performance**; scenes should include losses, close calls, victories, danger and changed relationships as appropriate.
6. The world exposes bounty hotspots and pursuit breakers with physics-informed behavior; forests, dam roads, tunnels, villages and city alleys enable different types of escapes.
7. [TBD] Exact assignment of rank levels to the five city regions; an agent **must not invent all 15 rival names, races or race order and call it approved**.

**Player experience:** meaningful rise with setbacks; personal relationships evolve alongside the racing empire campaign.

### ACT VI — “The Empire Answers Back”
**When:** advancing near the strongest ranks.  
**Thematic purpose:** The main enemy must feel like a powerful person, not an end-screen icon.

1. The former friend/rival is now the person who controls the powerful racing empire.
2. Progression toward him moves through rising opposition, mandatory boss challenges and story events. The rival's reactions to player actions should intensify.
3. The girlfriend's secret support and situation remain consistent with the betrayal mystery; any major reveal of coercion and truth needs the owner's approved sequence.
4. [TBD] Antagonist's exact network, lieutenants, holdings, police corruption (if any), and final act location are not canon.

**Player experience:** a slow-building confrontation with increasing emotional stakes.

### FINAL ACT — “Rise From Zero”
**When:** major final challenge(s).  
**Thematic purpose:** Make the confrontation repay the long story.

1. [CONFIRMED] The protagonist **confronts and defeats his former childhood friend and the racing empire** as the main revenge/comeback resolution.
2. [CONFIRMED] There should be **different endings/reactions based on performance/progress/actions**, *not mandatory Q&A choices during a race*.
3. [TBD] Exact final race sequence, canonical ending, forgiveness/retribution outcome, relationship outcome, antagonist fate and ending unlock algorithm. They must be collaboratively authored later—not guessed as facts.
4. Show the contrast between the childhood garage and the player's evolved racing identity, via cinematics and owned-car/garage continuity.

**Player experience:** closure with observable consequences and room for branch variation.

## Cinematic pacing and player feedback
- **Long scenes:** friendship foundations, major wins, betrayal, imprisonment, release, key story revelations, final confrontation.
- **Short scenes:** expert invitations, pre-race taunts, post-race reactions, girlfriend encounters, police busts, unlock milestones, garage handovers.
- **Between scenes:** world navigation, driving to location, changing car, garage work, cutscene-free pacing.
- **No forced dialogue quizzes while racing.** Reaction logic derives from race placement, time, crash count, heat, pursuit escape/busted, rank and prior events; see 02.
- Player must always understand **why** a story chapter/rank became accessible.

## Flow coherence checks for writers/AGO agents
- The childhood friendship is established *before* betrayal.
- Girlfriend is not introduced only near the finale: she has intro and ongoing presence.
- Betrayer's empire must develop while protagonist is imprisoned.
- Post-release protagonist is resource-constrained and must re-earn status.
- Ranked progression and reactions must feed the revenge arc, not be isolated unrelated events.
- **Ordinary police bust with fine is NOT the same event as the main prison betrayal**.
- Ending variations arise from recorded player behavior, not unexplained roulette or in-race quizzes.
- Unknown narrative detail is **TBD, not omitted and not invented**.
